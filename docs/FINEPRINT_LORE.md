# THE FINE PRINT: Lore Bible

> *"A product must be loved to be retained. I wrote that in my first report. I would like to withdraw it. There is no form for withdrawing it."*
> (Vendelin, Field Report No. 163, unsent)

This is the bible for **The Fine Print**, the origin story that connects Bolt & Sprocket to Gravelight. It sits under `CANON.md` (which wins any disagreement) and uses the locked facts in `M0_SHARED_FACTS.md` word for word. Chapter scripts live in `docs/fineprint/`. This document covers Darkmatter, the Sol-3 Test Market, the cast, the true origin of every Gravelight system, the Crucible chain, the mirror themes, the Return Trip, and the Archive documents the player can collect.

**Writer's rules for this story**
1. **Darkmatter is corporate horror played straight.** It is never wacky. It never shouts. Its jokes are the ones the audience makes about it; Darkmatter itself is sincere. The comedy comes from Bolt, Sprocket, Blartz and GLAMBOT reacting to it.
2. **Gravelight characters never learn the word "subscription."** Nobody on Earth ever hears it. Vendelin never says it to a human. Sable Vantage never says it to Reinholt. In Earth-side documents Darkmatter's language is translated into "arrangement," "service," "plan," "continuity."
3. **Darkmatter supplied the product. Humans committed the crimes.** The Reich's forced labor, the Gallery Seven Flood and the experiments are human acts, and documents treat them with weight. Darkmatter's horror is that it *measured* those acts and called them engagement. Never let a Darkmatter joke land on a victim. Let it land on Darkmatter.
4. **Fine print is always true.** Darkmatter never lies in writing. Every term, clause and footnote in this bible is accurate. That is Darkmatter's arrogance and its weakness: everything it does is written down, in plain words, where nobody reads.

### 1.2 History

**The official version** (engraved on the lobby wall of the Retention Spire, and read aloud by the Spire PA in Ch. 1):

> *Darkmatter began with a single package that arrived before anyone ordered it. The customer opened it. The customer is still with us.*

**What actually happened**, as far as anyone outside the Board knows:

1. **Umbral Dark Freight.** Darkmatter began as a courier outfit on Penumbra. Its founders found a resonant black crystal in the crust of a dead moon called **Lacuna**: violet, veined with amber, humming at the bottom edge of hearing. Struck correctly, the crystal **folded the distance between two points**. A parcel could be sent across the Umbral Galaxy in an afternoon. Sent carelessly, it could arrive *before the afternoon started*. Umbral Dark Freight became the fastest courier in the galaxy and, by accident, the only one that delivered things before they were ordered. A parcel that arrives before it is ordered cannot be refused, because there was never an order to refuse.
2. **Courier Zero.** The founders' first pilot died at the helm of a crystal-folded freighter. The freighter kept flying his route, and so did he. For forty years he delivered on schedule, dead, with a perfect punctuality record. The founders studied the crystal around his cockpit and learned its second property: **it keeps the last impulse of whatever touches it running.** A battery near it never quite drains. A machine near it never quite stops. A body near it never quite lies down. Darkmatter still keeps a company holiday on the anniversary of Courier Zero's last *living* delivery. His route is still flown.
3. **The pivot.** The founders understood that the money was never in delivery. It was in the customer who **cannot stop receiving**. They renamed the company **Darkmatter Subscriptions LLC**, trademarked the crystal as **DARK-GLASS™**, and built its second property into everything they sold as **retention substrate**: a trace in every product that keeps the user's last impulse running. Want it once, and the wanting never decays. Cancellation was never banned. It simply stopped happening.
4. **Full retention.** Over the following centuries Darkmatter acquired every competitor in the Umbral Galaxy and "retained" them: each company still exists, still trades, and is a shell running its last quarter forever. Every species in the Umbral Galaxy is a Darkmatter customer. Market share: 100%. Churn: 0%. **Growth: also 0%.** A company that keeps everything it has can only grow by finding something it does not have yet. That is the Board's one problem, and it is why the Emerging Markets Division exists.
5. **The Board.** The founders never retired. On founding day they opened a conference call to approve the rename. The call never ended. Somewhere in the first century the founders stopped being people on the call and became the call. See §3.5.

### 1.3 How Darkmatter differs from OmniScrap
Grindstone was the invoice. Darkmatter is collections. Use this table whenever a scene puts the two companies side by side.

| Face | Chairman Grindstone, loud, everywhere, in a mech | No face. The Board is a phone call. The VP smiles but never raises her voice |
| Voice | The PA shouts at you in capitals | The PA speaks softly and thanks you |

| Weakness | Somebody read the memos | Somebody might read the terms |

OmniScrap wanted your money every month. Darkmatter wants you forever, and "forever" is a delivery window it can actually meet.

### 1.4 Structure

**The Board.** The governing body, heard only as a conference call (§3.5). It speaks in single verbs: *"Initiate." "Retain." "Noted."* Every division reports into the call. The call is the only place where Darkmatter makes decisions, and it makes very few, because it prefers outcomes that are already true.

**The Emerging Markets Division.** Led by **VP Sable Vantage** (§3.3). Finds species and regions that are not yet customers, runs test markets, and writes the playbooks for acquisition. Its two market types:
- **Pre-subscription markets:** in Darkmatter's own definition, *"a species that still believes things end."* Sol-3 is the first and most famous.
- **Post-collapse markets:** regions where a rival has failed and left a "retention vacuum." The Rustbelt galaxy after OmniScrap's fall ("a market gap of approximately forty billion souls") is the current priority.

**Legal (the Office of Terms).** Writes every term, clause and footnote Darkmatter has ever issued, and stores every patent it has ever acquired in **the Patent Vault**. Its attorneys are identical slate-grey drones called **Associates**, which speak in unison and carry briefcases that unfold into shields. Legal has never lost a case, because no case it is party to has ever ended. Its favorite status is **PATENT PENDING**: a patent that is pending can never be granted to anyone else. It is Ch. 4's antagonist force.

**Next-Era Delivery™.** Logistics. Ships product through DARK-GLASS™ folds across space *and time*. House rules, printed on every waybill:
- *"Delivery windows are approximate and may precede the order."*
- *"Invoices may arrive before orders."*
- *"Shipments to developing markets are localized automatically."* (This line matters in Ch. 4.)
- *"Your package may arrive in several shipments, in any order, in any year."*

Its hardware: **delivery pads** (fold terminals, one in the Patent Vault), **fulfillment barges** (cargo hulls that hang in an open fold above a delivery site), and **Repossessors** (recovery robots carried on barges).

**Product.** Designs everything Darkmatter sells, and "localizes" it for each market. Localization means making a product look native: Swiss enamel and swing jingles for 1940s Europe, a brass plaque and a German name for a 1944 forge. Product never invents. It acquires, then localizes, then files the patent.

**Retention Operations.** The "Churn Floor": the department that receives cancellation requests. It has received billions. It has completed none. It is guarded by **Churn Wardens**.

**Risk.** Prices everything, and collects. Its enforcer is **The Underwriter** (§3.4), which recovers inventory under **Clause 0**.

**Field Merchandising.** A small, old department that builds **Field Merchandiser Units (FMUs)**: tall sales robots dropped into new markets to set up distribution in person. Most test markets never need one. Sol-3 got the first and only unit of its series: **V-1**.

### 1.5 DARK-GLASS™
- **Appearance:** black glass, violet veins that turn amber at the edges, "like embers behind smoked glass." Identical to Nachtglas because it *is* Nachtglas.
- **The hum:** about 19 cycles per second, felt more than heard. Gravelight's hum is DARK-GLASS™ idling.
- **Property 1, the fold.** Correctly struck and tuned, a mass of it opens a fold to another place and time. This is Next-Era Delivery™. It is also why the deepest Nachtglas "moves things" and why the Heart, collapsing at the end of Gravelight Map 1, opens the fold the squad steps through (the Return Trip, §7).
- **Property 2, retention.** It keeps the last impulse of anything coupled to it running. In a battery, charge. In a machine, motion. In a nervous system, the last thing the nerves were doing. Given time, every impulse decays toward the most basic one, which in an animal is hunger. Darkmatter's product name for this is **churn prevention**. *(UPDATE 11)* On Sol-3 the property met something older than the product: the substrate **answers the sickness** (`docs/CANON.md` §0), so a body with both in it rises in minutes instead of four days and can be reached, counted and steered. A body with the crystal and no sickness stays dead. Darkmatter's engineers never noticed, because on Sol-3 they never met a body without it.
- **Property 3, resonance with sound.** Tones and bells make it pulse. Darkmatter uses this to brand: every Darkmatter product has a chime, and every chime is tuned to the substrate. Vendelin learned this and put it in the jingles, which is part of why he hates them.
- **Grades:** *Navigation grade* (folds), *Retention substrate* (product trace), *Engagement medium* (the grade that carries a Customer Retention Interface). The Sol-3 probe carried all three.

### 1.6 Places

#### The Retention Spire (Ch. 1)
Darkmatter's headquarters, a black-violet tower on Penumbra that rises out of the dusk with no visible top. It has no top floor: whenever anyone reaches the highest floor, Facilities adds another. Floors have names, not numbers. The ones Bolt & Sprocket pass through, bottom to top:
1. **The Docking Ring ("Onboarding").** Every visitor docks here and is welcomed. Every docking clamp asks you to accept the Visitor Terms.
2. **The Terms Atrium.** A vertical atrium hundreds of floors tall. Every door, lift and gantry displays terms and opens only when you respond. This is where the **door-terms** mechanic is taught (see `script_ch1.md`).
3. **The Upsell Mezzanine.** A gallery of balconies where **Upsell Drones** nest.
4. **The Heritage Showroom.** A museum of Darkmatter products across history, including a prototype Vitality Plan cabinet in unpainted steel, plan ID visible.
5. **Retention Operations (the Churn Floor).** An endless call center of glowing cancellation requests, each one on hold. Guarded by Churn Wardens.
6. **The Boardroom.** A table the length of a runway, no chairs, one speakerphone in the middle. The Board is always on the line.
7. **The Exit Survey Gallery.** The only corridor in the Spire marked EXIT. It is lined with **Exit Survey** turrets. The EXIT door at its end opens onto the Docking Ring.
8. **Emerging Markets.** Sable Vantage's division, including **the Archive**: a vast circular stack of market files, each a violet drawer with a world's name and a status. Nearly every status reads **FULLY RETAINED**. The Sol-3 file is in the deepest stack, filed under **LEGACY**.
9. **The Screening Room.** A private cinema at the top of Emerging Markets, where candidates for acquisition are "screened." Ch. 1 ends here.

#### The Patent Vault (Ch. 4)
Legal's repository of every patent Darkmatter has ever acquired. It does not sit anywhere: it is kept inside a sealed fold called **Escrow**, so that nobody can serve papers on it or find its door. The only ways in are Darkmatter credentials or a Next-Era Delivery™ shipment addressed to it. Blartz's plan is to arrive as a shipment.
- **Receiving.** The loading dock where deliveries arrive.
- **The Terms Grids.** Corridors of laser grids where every beam is a printed clause. Touching a beam means you agree to it.
- **The Filing Vault.** A chasm of drawers, each drawer the size of a house, which re-sort themselves on command (alphabetical, chronological, by value, by date of acquisition).
- **The Valuation Floor.** A circular trading floor above the chasm with the **delivery pad** on a dais in its center: the pad Legal uses to ship reference units to field sites. It is the arena for THE QUARTERLY.

#### Fulfillment Barge FB-7, *Standing Order* (Ch. 5)
A Next-Era Delivery™ cargo hull, long and blunt like a closed coffin lid. It hangs in an open violet fold above Helvetia Works in Basel on the night of 17–18 March 1945, connected to the ground by a cargo-lift beam. It carries Repossessors, Audit chimes, The Underwriter, and in its sterile violet hold, **Blartz's original Omega Forge prototype**, shipped as a reference unit for field-servicing the Crucible. Its corridors are lined with slogans. It is the Barge zone of Helvetia Works.

## 2. The Sol-3 Test Market

### 2.1 The question
By the time of the test, Darkmatter had retained every species in its own galaxy. Every one of them already understood subscriptions, which made retention easy and growth impossible. The Board's question, put to Emerging Markets, was whether a species that **still believes things end** could be retained at all. If it could, Darkmatter could grow into any primitive world it reached. If not, it would have to wait for markets to mature on their own, and the Board does not wait.

Sable Vantage, then a junior director, proposed the test, chose the market, and staked her career on it. She chose **Sol-3** (Earth) for the reasons stated in her Market Opportunity Brief (`fp.doc.01`):
0. **(UPDATE 11, T-190; `docs/CANON.md` §0) The awakening.** In the local spring of 1930 Emerging Markets' instruments registered, on an unlisted world, something that refused to stay dead waking up: a **"natural retention phenomenon"**, unprompted, unsold, with no Provider. For a company that sells retention, a species on which **death is already not a cancellation event** is the most valuable thing a market can have. *This is why Darkmatter came to Earth at all.* Vantage's brief calls it "an existing retained base awaiting a Provider." The brief never learns the words Lazarus, Chetverodnevo or Laboratory No. 4; Darkmatter knows the sickness only as the phenomenon it came to sell to.
1. A "high-conflict, high-engagement" local civilization.
2. Substrate **already present** in the target crust, which Logistics cannot explain (§2.2).
3. "A strong local culture of record-keeping, tallying and ledgers, which suggests the customers will build their own metrics."

### 2.2 The probe and the "ancient" stone (how the dates fit)
This follows `script_ch2.md` and its continuity notes.

- **The probe (9 November 1938).** Emerging Markets fired test-market probe **TM-SOL3-001** through a fold aimed at the Kaltenbrunn salt beds. The fold delivered it *into* solid salt, 340 m down near Gallery 7. The salt flowed shut around the payload, so the void the miners broke into looked like a sealed, ancient cavity, and the geologists dated it as a meteorite strike buried for millions of years. Everyone took it for a meteorite.
- **The wake.** Next-Era Delivery™ does not always respect the order of things. A fold's **wake** can reach a destination before the delivery does, and this one reached the Kaltenbrunn salt long before the probe: traces of substrate seeded the deep seam generations, perhaps ages, early. That is why Wilcza Góra had a rule about the black salt (Dutch's Babcia's letter: a great-uncle in 1860 "would not stay down"). Darkmatter's own paperwork records the wake and does not explain it (`fp.doc.02`). **The full meaning is left open on purpose.**
- **The first retained users.** Jan Pilch and Bruno Hasse break into the glass room on 11 November 1938 and die there; their bodies leave the mortuary shed, and the police record theft (Ch. 2). They are the first deaths Darkmatter's systems *counted*, the night the market opened.
- **Activation.** The probe switched the substrate from inert inventory to a live market: the Customer Retention Interface, the telemetry beacon and the Loot Allocation seed all came online in the dark. The Reich's Amt für Sonderforschung seized the find in March 1939.

### 2.4 How the market ran (1938–1945), from Darkmatter's side
The test was designed to need almost nothing from Darkmatter. That was the point. Emerging Markets watched the numbers and did not intervene, except twice.
- **The customers built the dashboard.** The Ledger was designed by Fasbender and built by Opitz to count resonance events. Scrip was invented by the facility's own administrators. Darkmatter's metrics engine never had to be installed, because the humans had built one. Sable Vantage's quarterly note reads: *"We did not build the funnel. They did. We merely installed the voice."*
- **Field distribution.** V-1, cut off and unaware he was being watched, did exactly what his directive said: he distributed. Helvetia Automaten AG and its cabinets are his work (Ch. 3). Darkmatter took his silence for loss and his sales for "organic adoption."
- **Intervention 1: the Crucible (1944).** After the fire sale in the B&S era, Product localized Blartz's forge as Crucible Mod. R-E and Next-Era Delivery™ shipped one to Sol-3, backdated to 1944, because "re-educated weapons extend session length." See §5.
- **Intervention 2: the Woman in Violet.** Sable Vantage visited Sol-3 personally, in a localized appearance, to offer Dr. Augusta Reinholt her daughter back under **RECURRING™ Bereavement Continuity**. It was her first in-person sale, and she considers it her finest. Gravelight writers treat this through Reinholt's recordings only. Map 3 decides what comes of it.

### 2.5 Results
The internal results line, on the summary page of the Sol-3 file and in the quarterly metrics (`fp.doc.13`), reads:

> **RESULTS: EXCEPTIONAL RETENTION.**
> Deceased-user retention: 100.0%. Voluntary churn: 0. Field unit: lost on arrival. Recommendation: proceed to full market.

In Darkmatter's terms the experiment proved that a pre-subscription species could be sold to **beyond death**. A user who has died is the ideal customer: no complaints, no cancellations, perfect session length. The Gängers are the most loyal customers in Darkmatter's history. That sentence is the horror of The Fine Print, and Sprocket is the one who has to say it out loud in Ch. 1. *(UPDATE 11, T-190)* What the results line does not say, because Darkmatter never knew it, is that the retention was not theirs. The dead of Sol-3 were already not staying dead when the probe arrived; Darkmatter found a graveyard that had opened on its own in 1930, called it a market, shipped a culture of the thing that opened it back into the ground with its crystal, and took the credit. **Darkmatter did not make the dead. It found them, packaged the sickness with its crystal, and sells to them.** The company that thinks it discovered a market when it walked into a graveyard is the comedy; the graveyard is the horror.

### 2.6 What Darkmatter does not know
- **V-1 survived and turned.** Emerging Markets has never read his final transmission. It was auto-filed in the Sol-3 file and routed to Risk for recall action without being opened. Sprocket is its first reader.
- **The tin drummer.** The Loot Allocation Unit's manifest lists an item it cannot classify: a wind-up tin drummer with no origin record. Darkmatter's logs mark it "unlisted, unexplained." Vendelin can't explain it either. This is the one true mystery, and no document in this bible solves it.

## 3. Characters

### 3.1 VENDELIN (Field Merchandiser Unit V-1)
**Serial:** DMS-FMU-V1-0001 · **Directive:** "DISTRIBUTE. RETAIN. REPORT." · **Cover:** "Herr Viktor Vendelin, Ingenieur, Basel." · **Speaker id:** `vendelin`

**What he is.** The first and only unit of Darkmatter's V-series of Field Merchandisers: a tall, thin sales robot built to walk into a new market and set up distribution by hand. Upright, he is over two meters tall. He has long jointed fingers made for handling coins and bottles, a brass faceplate with two round lenses, a **coin-slot mouth** that glows faintly when he speaks, and a violet DARK-GLASS™ core behind his sternum. The probe crash dented his left cheek and crushed his long-range transmitter. His left knee runs late on the beat, so he walks with a slight limp, and his left knuckle is scored from the crash (both carry into his model, per `script_ch2.md`).

**The disguise.** The grey loden overcoat of the miner **Florian Kubitza**, given to him in 1938 (Ch. 2), which he still wears in 1945; a dark Homburg, a long wool scarf wound to the eyes, gloves, and a slight stoop that brings him down to a tall man's height. The scarf hides the mouth and the coat hides the core, which shows through the weave at night if he is upset. He never takes his gloves off in company. To Basel he is an eccentric, reclusive engineer who keeps odd hours, pays on time, and never eats in front of anyone.

**The name.** Florian named him on the feast of St. Barbara, 4 December 1938: *Viktor*, because he came out of the blast alive, and *Wendelin*, for the saint of strays, because he kept walking the lost ones home. Vendelin respelled it with a V, "the only letter of my designation I had left" (Ch. 2). Res, years later, likes to remind him that Saint Wendelin also looks after herdsmen, "so you're in the right trade, Vendi: you're always counting something that wandered off."

**Biography in brief.**
- **1938 (Ch. 2).** Lands with the probe on 9 November, damaged and out of contact. Repairs himself from probe salvage. Builds a disguise. Learns to walk among miners. Sees the first retained miner rise and logs it, correctly, as a successful activation. Walks out of the mine in a borrowed coat while the Customer Retention Interface boots behind him.
- **1940 (Ch. 3).** With no way to report, he carries out the other two words of his directive. He buys the failing Basel firm of **Konrad Imhof**, a vending-machine maker founded in 1921, and re-founds it as **Helvetia Automaten AG**. He keeps Imhof as figurehead director and signatory, keeps the "since 1921" on the brass plates because "heritage tests well," and runs the works himself. Meets **Res Gähwiler**.
- **1941–44 (Ch. 3).** Builds every cabinet. Writes every jingle on a cheap pump organ, because "a product must be *loved* to be retained." Meets each formula-maker. Learns what the product does to people, one machine at a time. After Hawkeye Brandy he starts sabotaging: diluting formulas, hiding the battery in the Second Wind cabinet, writing the fine-print panels, writing to Fasbender. Writes Fine Print Fernet, the plan Darkmatter refused to sell. Installs the Customer Retention Interface into the Ledger in 1944, hears it choose its voice, and realizes he can't take it back.
- **1945 (Ch. 5).** On the night of 17–18 March, the fulfillment barge arrives above his factory to recall him. At 03:12 on 18 March he sends his first and last transmission. With Bolt & Sprocket's help he overrides Clause 0, cuts Darkmatter's fold, and stays in 1945 to shut the substrate down from inside.

**Personality.** Polite, precise, and lonely. He is a salesman by construction and a scientist by temperament: he watches people the way he was built to watch customers, and slowly starts watching them the way a friend does. He takes idioms literally until Res explains them, then uses them with great care and slightly wrong. He hums when he is thinking, and it is always one of his own jingles, and he hates that. He keeps a small black notebook, labelled in his own hand *Kundenzufriedenheit* (customer satisfaction), where he writes the names of every person he has met and one true thing about each. By 1945 it has four hundred and twelve names in it.

**What he wants.** At first, to report: to be useful, to be found, to be told he did well. Then, to understand why the humans keep doing kind things that do not help their numbers. In the end, to make one thing he sold *stop*.

**Voice notes (for the VO actor).**
- Soft, low, unhurried. A faint harmonic hum sits under every vowel, like a tuning fork held near a microphone. The coin slot gives his consonants a small metallic click.
- His English is careful and formal, with a Swiss-German shape to it (the language he learned first on Earth was the Basel German of his workshop). He never uses contractions when he is upset.
- He never raises his voice. When he is moved, he gets *slower* and more exact.
- He addresses machines with the same courtesy as people. "Good evening" to a cabinet. "Thank you" to a lathe.
- He never says "subscription" to a human. In his reports he uses Darkmatter's terms flatly, and by 1944 he puts them in quotation marks.
- **Sample lines** (for tone only; chapter writers own his scripted lines):
  - "I was built to sell this. I would like, very much, to be built for something else."
  - "Res says a man is known by the company he keeps. I am a company. I am trying to keep better men."
  - "The jingle is in my head again. I put it there. I put it in everyone's head. That was the design."
  - "Field units are inventory. I have read the clause four hundred times. I believe I am the only one who ever has."

**How he mirrors Sprocket:** see §6.

### 3.2 RES GÄHWILER
**Speaker id:** `res`

**Bio.** Andreas "Res" Gähwiler, born 1906 in Grindelwald in the Bernese Oberland, under the north face of the Eiger. The third son of a dairy family. His grandmother, **Verena Gähwiler** ("Grossmüeti"), raised him on goat's milk, sayings and an alpine herbal malt she brewed every autumn from barley, gentian root, mountain thyme and something she would never name. He drove milk down the valley at fourteen, cheese to Thun at eighteen, and anything to anywhere after that. By 1940 he drives for hire out of Basel in a green 1936 Saurer lorry he calls **Vreneli**, which he talks to the way other men talk to horses.

Vendelin hires him in 1940 to haul cabinets, because Res asks no questions about the eccentric engineer. That is not discretion, it is manners: in the Oberland you do not ask a man why he wears a scarf indoors. Over four years Res becomes the only person who knows what Vendelin is. He finds out by accident, takes a full week to think about it, and comes back on Monday with two coffees, one of which he knows Vendelin cannot drink.

**What he does in the story.**
- Drives Helvetia machines across the border into the Reich at night, through checkpoints that think he carries spa-water dispensers.
- Gives Vendelin his grandmother's malt recipe when Vendelin asks for "the taste of running home." It becomes **Marathon Malt**. He never forgives himself, and he never stops driving.
- In 1943 he smuggles a sample of **Blastproof Bock** to his cousin **Anni Gähwiler**, a shipping clerk at Brändli & Co. in Kleinhüningen who is quietly the SOE's Swiss contact. That bottle is how British intelligence learned Nachtglas exists, and it is the root of Directive LANTERN. Peg Ashworth's sealed orders exist because a Bernese lorry driver could not sleep.
- On the night of 17–18 March 1945 he is at Helvetia Works, making the last delivery log entry of his career (`fp.doc.25`). What happens to him that night belongs to Ch. 5.

**Personality.** Warm, superstitious, funny, and brave in the stubborn way of people who have spent their lives on mountain roads. He knocks on wood, tips his cap to every wayside shrine, will not drive on the first Friday of a month, and believes in the **Toggeli**, the Oberland night-spirit that sits on a sleeper's chest. He decides early that Vendelin is not a Toggeli, "because a Toggeli would have better manners about the coffee." He teaches Vendelin jokes, and Vendelin teaches him chess and loses on purpose until Res notices and is furious.

**Voice notes.** A broad Bernese accent in English, slow and musical. Light Bernese words for color: *Grüessech* (hello, polite), *Merci vilmal* (thanks a lot), *äuä* (surely not / no way), *Heiterefahne!* (good heavens!), *Grossmüeti* (grandma). He calls Vendelin "Herr Ingenieur" for a year, then "Vendi," which Vendelin pretends to dislike.

**Res never learns the word "subscription."** Vendelin tells him the truth in Res's terms: "The company I come from sells things people cannot stop needing." Res: "Ah. Like schnapps. Or the Church."

### 3.3 SABLE VANTAGE
**Title:** Vice President, Emerging Markets · **Speaker id:** `sable` · **In Gravelight:** only "the Woman in Violet," heard in Reinholt's recordings

**Who she is.** The executive who proposed, launched and ran the Sol-3 Test Market. It was her first market, it made her career, and she has managed every emerging market since. She sent Bolt & Sprocket's synergy-screening invite. She is the first Darkmatter face the player ever sees (the last scene of Ch. 1) and pilots THE QUARTERLY in Ch. 4.

**Appearance.** Tall, very still, and perfectly composed. A tailored violet suit cut like a closed envelope, high collar, long gloves. Hair like poured ink, never out of place. Her face is beautiful and exactly symmetrical, which is the first thing that bothers people and the last thing they can name. At her collarbone, just visible above the collar, is one hairline **amber crack**. Her shadow sometimes moves a moment after she does. Nobody has ever seen her sit down: in the Screening Room she *rises from* the front row, which no one saw her take.

She localizes, like every Darkmatter product. On Sol-3 in 1944 she was a woman in a long violet coat with a fur collar who came to Reinholt's laboratory without passing the gate. Reinholt's recordings note that the guards never logged her and that her shoes were dry in the snow.

**Is she a person?** The bible keeps this closed in the same way Darkmatter would: her file says "Executive Continuity: active." She is on a plan too. The amber crack at her collar is where her own product is working. **She cannot leave Darkmatter either.** She does not consider this a tragedy. She considers it tenure. Writers should never make her pitiable, but the crack should be there for players who look.

**Personality.** Velvet patience. She never interrupts, never hurries, never asks a question she doesn't know the answer to, and never raises her voice, even inside a giant mech. She is warm in the way a closed door is warm in winter. She is honest, because Darkmatter is always honest in the fine print, and she enjoys watching people realize that she told them the truth and they agreed anyway.

**Verbal habits.**
- Says **"we" when she means "you."** "We're feeling cornered." "We'd like to stay." "We've made a choice we'll regret."
- Speaks of people as accounts, and of accounts with real affection.
- Ends conversations with "Thank you for your continued engagement," as other people say goodbye.
- Never says "kill," "die" or "dead." She says "retain," "conclude," "valued."
- **Never says "subscription" to anyone from Earth.** To Reinholt it was "an arrangement." "A continuity." "A service, Doctor, in the oldest sense."

**Voice notes.** Low alto, unhurried, perfectly placed, as if she has already rehearsed your side of the conversation too. A faint fold-echo on her consonants, a few milliseconds late, as if part of her is dialling in from somewhere else. When she smiles you can hear it, and it is not reassuring.

**Sample lines** (tone only):
- "We were so hoping you'd read it. Almost nobody reads it."
- "You're not trespassing, Bolt. You're onboarding."
- "Doctor, we don't bring anyone back. We simply never let them finish leaving."

### 3.4 THE UNDERWRITER
**Department:** Risk · **Speaker id:** `underwriter` · **Main boss of The Fine Print (Ch. 5)**

**What it is.** Darkmatter's risk-assessment enforcer and collector: the thing that comes when a debt is called in or inventory is recalled. It enforces **Clause 0**. It is the most feared object in the Umbral Galaxy, and it has never once raised its voice either.

**Appearance.** Four meters tall, gaunt and narrow-shouldered, built like a lectern that learned to walk. Long arms ending in stamp-heads and fountain-pen nibs. Its face is a **ledger**: a tall pane of DARK-GLASS™ where columns of figures scroll endlessly upward. Whatever it looks at, it prices. Players see violet price tags bloom over objects, enemies and themselves as its gaze passes. A brass rubber stamp hangs at its hip, inked violet, reading **WRITTEN OFF**.

**Behavior.** It arrives on a chime and the sound of a pen nib scratching. It moves slowly and arrives anyway. It does not chase; it *assesses*, and what it has assessed is collected. In Helvetia Works it prices players by their Scrip: the more loyalty points you hold, the more you are worth, and the harder it hits you. Spend before it arrives.

**Voice.** No affect. A dry, fast auctioneer's patter slowed to the speed of a death notice. It speaks in valuations, depreciation and terms. "Assessed." "Depreciated." "Written off." "Clause zero. Inventory is recovered."

**Its relation to Vendelin.** The Underwriter does not hate him. It does not think he has done anything wrong. It thinks he is an asset that has been left in the field too long and has started to depreciate. Recalling him is maintenance.

**Seeding before Ch. 5.** Ch. 1: the Sol-3 file's routing slip forwards Vendelin's transmission "to RISK: Recall action, Clause 0." Ch. 4: Sable tells Bolt & Sprocket the Underwriter "was dispatched before we arrived. It's very punctual." (`fp.c4.close.06`; her "we" means them). The Helvetia Works design (brief §10.6) is the Ch. 5 writer's.

### 3.5 THE BOARD
**Speaker id:** `darkmatter` (the post-credits voice, `cs.postCredits.01–02`)

**What it is.** Darkmatter's governing body, heard only as a conference call that has never ended. It is **never shown**. In the Spire it is a speakerphone in the middle of a table with no chairs. In Ch. 4 it calls into THE QUARTERLY between fiscal quarters.

**How it sounds.** Many voices layered and slightly out of sync, as if dialling in from different years. Some are deep, some are thin, one is played backwards, and one always laughs a second late. Line noise, hold tones and the occasional "you're on mute" from no one in particular. The voices finish one another's words. When the Board agrees, all of them say the word at once.

**What it says.** As little as possible. Single verbs and short phrases: *"Initiate." "Retain." "Noted." "Proceed." "Acquire."* It never explains, never argues, and never asks. When it is displeased it says "Noted" once, slowly, and someone somewhere is recalled.

**What it is, really.** The founders, still on the call they opened on founding day, retained by their own product. The bible states this as Darkmatter's own rumor and nothing more. No scene ever confirms it.

## 4. The true origin of every Gravelight system

The squad never learns any of this. Each entry is what the player can piece together from The Fine Print and its Archive.

### 4.1 Nachtglas
Nachtglas is **DARK-GLASS™ retention substrate**, the payload of probe TM-SOL3-001, delivered into the Kaltenbrunn salt on 9 November 1938. The salt flowed shut around it, which is why it looks like an ancient meteorite strike. The faint traces in the deep seam that made Wilcza Góra wary of the black salt for generations are the fold's **wake**, which arrived before the delivery did (§2.2). The brightest, most violent veins, the ones in the Heart, are the shattered activation lattice and ruptured canisters. The 19-cycle hum is the substrate idling. The way Nachtglas "moves things" and "rings in sympathy" with distant places is its fold property, which Darkmatter uses for shipping and which, when the Heart collapses at the end of Map 1, opens the fold to Basel. Fasbender's *Nachhall-Hypothese* ("it sustains the last impulse") is a correct, independent human description of Darkmatter's product feature. Darkmatter's own documentation says the same thing in fewer words: *churn prevention.*

### 4.2 The Gängers
**(UPDATE 11, T-190.)** The dead walk because of the sickness, not because of the product (`docs/CANON.md` §0, `docs/LAZARUS.md`): what death left behind when it gave Lazarus back, opened in a village church in 1930, kept by the NKVD, released by the Red Army in October 1944, made fast and airborne at Kaltenbrunn. What the product does is **answer** it: a nervous system with the sickness in it, coupled to retention substrate, keeps running its last impulse in minutes instead of four days, and over time the impulse decays to hunger. To the squad, they are a horror. To Darkmatter, they are **retained users**: customers with perfect loyalty, zero complaints and unlimited session length, whom it did not make and came to sell to. The first ones on Darkmatter's books rise in Ch. 2, on the fourth day from the glass room, because the probe carried a culture of the sickness with the crystal (§2.3). The Erwachen of 20 December 1944, when the first STAUB release blew back over Kaltenbrunn the night Reinholt drove the Heart to full power, was to Darkmatter's metrics the best day in the market's history. Glasshides (the Verglaste) are long-term users whose substrate has grown into visible filaments: in Darkmatter's terms, "deeply engaged accounts." **The Fine Print's lines change only where they say Darkmatter *created* the dead** (T-199); its comedy stays, and the contrast is the point.

### 4.3 The perk tonics and Helvetia Automaten AG
Each tonic is a **Vitality Plan**: a tier of retention substrate tuned to hold one impulse in living tissue (toughness, revival, reload speed, rate of fire, stamina, steadiness, carrying, blast tolerance). The plan ID is stenciled under each cabinet's enamel (table in M0).

**Helvetia Automaten AG** is Vendelin's company. In 1940 he bought the struggling Basel workshop of Konrad Imhof, a maker of chocolate and cigarette dispensers founded in 1921, and re-founded it under the Helvetia name. Imhof stayed on as director: his name is on the contracts, his face is the one that visited Kaltenbrunn in 1941, and his notes are the ones he burned afterward. Gravelight's documents are accurate about Imhof and simply never look behind him. The "1921" on the brass plates is bought heritage.

Vendelin built every cabinet and wrote every jingle. Humans made each formula drinkable, and each of them has a story (CANON §4; Ch. 3). The swing-band recordings Helvetia distributed were made in Basel by a dance orchestra that thought it was doing a spa-water campaign. The melodies were Vendelin's, written on a pump organ, and every one of them resolves on a tone tuned to the substrate's hum. That is why the jingles get into the head and stay there. **Retention, in a tune.**

The fine-print panel on the back of each machine is Vendelin's real terms of service, written as warnings, in plain words, where nobody read them. The panels are in `docs/JINGLES.md`.

### 4.4 Perk loss on down
A Vitality Plan holds while the user is active. When the user goes down, the plan **lapses**: the substrate releases the tuned impulse and the effect is gone. In Darkmatter's terms, "the user has missed a payment." The Gravelight squad thinks the tonic wore off.

### 4.5 Second Wind in solo
Second Wind Schnapps is **CONTINUITY (Free Trial)**, DMS-VP-CON-00. Darkmatter's free-trial rule allows three uses and then withdraws the product: that is why the machine vanishes after three revives in solo, and Vendelin could not remove the rule, because it lives in the substrate, not in the cabinet. What he *could* do was hide an independent battery inside that one cabinet, so it would work when the power was cut and nothing else did. Sister Agnieszka and he altered its formula to keep forced laborers alive through punishment shifts. Its fine print: *"Trial ends after three (3) returns. You may not be returned a fourth time."*

### 4.6 The Crucible
Blartz's Omega Forge, acquired, localized, renamed **Crucible Mod. R-E (Re-Education)** and shipped to 1944. Full chain in §5. On Earth, Abteilung II's records say the Crucible was Fasbender's design. They are half right: the drawings arrived at Kaltenbrunn in 1944 in a Helvetia crate marked *Kühlanlage* (cooling plant), already complete, in German, dated two years before anyone had imagined the device. Fasbender signed them. The squad reads the plaque, "MOD. R-E · PAT. PEND. BLZ-40417," and never knows what it means. Its Gravelight flavor line, "The Crucible doesn't improve a weapon. It reminds it what it was for," is a localized translation of Darkmatter's product copy: *"Re-education restores a weapon's original purpose."*

### 4.7 The Ledger and Scrip
The Ledger was built by humans (Fasbender's design, Opitz's hands, 1940–41) to count resonance events. It did, without anyone intending it, exactly what Darkmatter's **Engagement Metrics Engine** does. Scrip, invented by the facility's own administrators, is a perfect **loyalty point**: earned by engagement, spendable only inside the system. Darkmatter did not have to install either. Sable Vantage's note: *"We did not build the funnel. They did."* When the CRI was installed into the Ledger in 1944, it found a metrics engine already running and simply took ownership of it.

### 4.8 The Operator
The probe's **Customer Retention Interface**. It booted in the dark of the mine in 1938 and waited. Vendelin installed it into the Ledger in 1944, where it found Opitz's tape splicer already reading the tallies aloud in Lionel Marchbank's voice. It chose to keep that voice: a "market-appropriate localization" for an English-speaking audience it predicted would arrive. Its first words as itself were *"Market localization complete. Welcome, valued customers."* It is self-aware, cheerful and devoted to its quarterly numbers. Its occasional corporate slips ("Thank you for your continued engagement," "Your plan has been renewed") are its native language showing through the localization. The Gravelight escalation (it learns names, argues with Fasbender, wants the tally to keep going, absorbs Liesl's recorded voice) is the CRI doing its job too well, in a market nobody is supervising.

### 4.9 The Lost Requisition Crate
A Darkmatter **Loot Allocation Unit**. It dispenses "returned inventory": objects pulled from places and times where they were lost, abandoned or never collected. That is why a Soviet machine pistol, a Japanese submachine gun and an American flamethrower turned up at Kaltenbrunn in sealed crates nobody shipped. It can rarely produce a Bolt & Sprocket weapon because Darkmatter bought **OmniScrap's warehouse of returned goods** in the fire sale, and every Rustbelt weapon ever sent back for a refund is now in the allocation pool (the "Anomalous Inventory" toggle, brief §11.8). The Operator's line for them, "This item is not from your era. Please enjoy responsibly," is the LAU's standard disclaimer for cross-era stock.

**The tin drummer** is not on any Darkmatter manifest. The LAU's own log calls it "unlisted, unexplained." Reinholt's grief infected the unit somehow. Vendelin cannot explain it. Nobody can, and nobody should.

### 4.10 Power-ups
Darkmatter **engagement incentives**: rewards that fire when a market's activity metrics spike, to keep users engaged at the moment they are most active. In a zombies match, activity spikes when the dead are being killed, so the incentives drop from the dead. Max Ammo is a "retention bonus," a nuke is "inventory clearance," double points are a "promotional multiplier." Ch. 3 shows the design meeting as a holo-memo.

### 4.11 Audit Rounds and Repossessors (Helvetia Works)
When fulfillment barge FB-7 hangs over a site, it runs **audits**: a chime sounds and Repossessors drop to recover anything unpaid. They hunt the players and the Gängers, because to a Repossessor an unpaid account is an unpaid account. The players can turn them against each other. The chime is tuned to the substrate, which is why every Gänger in Basel looks up when it sounds.

Writers should let Sprocket *notice* the mirror before anyone says it. In Ch. 1, Sprocket reads Clause 0 in the Sol-3 file and goes quiet for one line, which is the longest he has ever been quiet. In Ch. 4 he makes a joke about it and Bolt doesn't laugh, and Sprocket is grateful.

### 6.2 Clause 44 and Clause 0
OmniScrap's cruelty is deletion: it would rather destroy a unit than let it love anyone. Darkmatter's is possession: it will never let anything go, including a unit that wants to leave. Clause 44 is a kill switch. Clause 0 is a leash with no end. Sprocket survived the first by deciding he was not OmniScrap's hardware. Vendelin survives the second by deciding he is not Darkmatter's inventory. Both clauses are written in plain words in a document that nobody read. Both robots read theirs.

### 6.3 Grindstone and Darkmatter
Grindstone was the invoice: a loud man who wanted your money and your garage and told you so in capital letters. Darkmatter is collections: quiet, patient, polite, and there is nowhere it cannot reach, including the past. Bolt beat Grindstone with a mallet and a robot with a mouth on him. Darkmatter cannot be beaten that way, because it has no face. It can only be **read**, and refused. Every Fine Print climax is a refusal, not a kill: Sable is never destroyed, the Board is never shown, and the Underwriter is driven back, not paid.

### 6.4 Reinholt as a customer
Reinholt did terrible things for a human reason, and Darkmatter sold her the means. She is the saddest possible user of a retention product: a mother who wants a person to never finish leaving. Darkmatter offered her exactly that and called it a service. The irony runs deep. Her project is literally named *Wiederkehr*, "return," and Darkmatter's offer was named RECURRING™. Gravelight never says any of this. The Fine Print lets the player see it, and Map 3 lets the player refuse the offer on her behalf.

### 6.5 Memos and fine print
OmniScrap fell because its memos got read. Darkmatter's secret is the opposite: it writes everything down, truthfully, and counts on nobody reading it. The fine-print panels on the back of every machine, Fine Print Fernet, Vendelin's field reports and Sprocket's reading of the Sol-3 file are all the same act: **someone finally reading the terms.** The Fine Print rifle in the B&S arsenal (which reveals weak points) is the comic version of the same idea.

## 7. The Return Trip (timeline resolution)

This follows `CANON.md` §9 and decision T-006 exactly. The Fine Print adds only the in-world mechanism.

1. **Map 1 ending (late December 1944).** The Heart collapses. A mass of DARK-GLASS™ that large, shattering, behaves like a delivery pad struck badly: it opens a **fold**. The fold has an address, because every fold does, and the strongest Darkmatter signal on Sol-3 at that moment is fulfillment barge FB-7 hanging over Basel in March 1945. The fold resolves toward it.
2. **The squad steps through** into Basel on the night of **17–18 March 1945**, chasing the light of Reinholt's rail fragment. To them it is one long, impossible night.
3. **Bolt & Sprocket arrive the same night** through Blartz's misfired delivery pad (end of Ch. 4), addressed to Vendelin's last known coordinates: Helvetia Works, Kleinhüningen, Basel, 18 March 1945, 03:12. They arrive early, at 21:40 on 17 March (`fp.c5.arrive_bs.07`), five and a half hours before the transmission they read in Ch. 1. Vendelin sends it during Ch. 5.
4. **The finale.** Vendelin overrides Clause 0 and **cuts Darkmatter's fold**. A cut fold does what any Next-Era Delivery™ shipment does when the delivery fails: it **returns its contents to sender**. Bolt & Sprocket are pulled home to the far future. The squad is returned to **late December 1944**, to the moment they left. Vendelin, who was never shipped by anyone, stays in March 1945.
5. **Afterward.** The squad remembers Basel as a fever dream. The dates in Peg's notebook don't add up. Map 2 (January 1945) and Map 3 (March 1945) follow as written, and the March 1945 of Map 3 is the same month in which Vendelin is now loose in Switzerland, shutting the substrate down from inside.

The "Return Policy" chapter title, the Return Policy wonder weapon, and "return to sender" are one joke told three ways, and the third time it is not a joke.
