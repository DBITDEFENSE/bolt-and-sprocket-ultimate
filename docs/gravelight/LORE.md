# GRAVELIGHT — Story Bible

> *"Ladies and gentlemen, a very good evening, and welcome back to the Ledger. Tonight's tally is looking simply marvellous."*
> — The Operator

This is the reference for all story, VO, collectibles, and Easter egg writing. Names and IDs follow `docs/CANON.md`. Things marked **[OPEN]** are deliberately unresolved, and writers should not close them early.

---

## 1. Tone

A lost war film that slowly becomes a ghost story. The opening hours feel like a survival picture: cold, mud, too few bullets, gallows jokes. The further down the squad goes, the more the place stops behaving like a war. It reads as grim pulp horror played straight, with the humor coming from the characters, never at the expense of the dead.

Rules for writers:
- **The dead were people.** Every Gänger was a guard, a miner, a technician, or a prisoner. Documents name them. Jokes are about the situation, never about the victims.
- **No one explains everything.** Fasbender knows a lot and lies a little. Reinholt is right about some things. The Operator is never fully reliable.
- **Real history is the backdrop, not the subject.** Forced labor and the occupation of Poland are depicted with weight. No real atrocity is used as spectacle, and no real person appears.
- **Nachtglas is the only saturated thing in the world,** in art and in story. Wherever there is color, there is something wrong.

## 2. Nachtglas

### 2.1 What it is (as the characters understand it)
Nachtglas ("night-glass") is a black, glassy mineral shot through with violet veins that turn amber at the edges, like embers seen through smoked glass. It was found on the night of **9 November 1938**, 340 m below the surface in the Kaltenbrunn salt mine. *(§11 amendment: the date was "March 1938," moved to match the locked probe-landing date.)* A tremor shook Gallery 7, and the crew cutting there broke into a sealed void the salt had grown around. The geologists found no entry channel and concluded it was the remains of a meteorite strike, sunk and buried in the Zechstein salt beds roughly 250 million years ago.

What the scientists of 1938–44 believed, in their own terms:
- **It resonates.** Raw Nachtglas emits a continuous low hum, about 19 cycles per second. That's at the very bottom edge of hearing, so people feel it more than hear it: as pressure behind the eyes, as unease, as a sense that someone is standing just behind them. Large masses hum louder and will "sing" (harmonics at 38 and 57 cps) when disturbed.
- **It remembers impulses.** Fasbender's working theory is the *Nachhall-Hypothese* (the "echo hypothesis"). Nachtglas resonance couples to the electrical activity of nervous tissue. In dead tissue it **sustains the last impulse the nerves carried**, like a struck bell that refuses to stop ringing. A body killed mid-stride keeps walking. A body that died starving keeps reaching for food. Given time and enough resonance, every impulse decays toward the most basic one: **hunger.**
- **It spreads along nerves.** In heavily exposed bodies, microscopic Nachtglas filaments grow along nerve paths. They show as glowing cracks on the skin, spreading out from the eyes and the hands. Bodies left near raw ore for months become *Verglaste*, "the glazed" (Glasshides).
- **It enhances living tissue, temporarily.** Diluted to a few parts per million in a carrier liquid, Nachtglas briefly "tunes" living nerves and muscle toward an impulse chosen by the formula: endurance, reflex, steadiness, toughness. This is the basis of the **perk tonics**. Effects last hours. Repeated doses accumulate (see §2.3).
- **It responds to sound.** Bells, struck metal, and certain tones make it pulse. A bronze bell tuned to the right note can *shatter* saturated tissue. This is the principle behind the Totenglocke and the reason the Salt Chapel bell became the facility alarm.
- **It moves things.** The deepest masses of Nachtglas bend space a little: objects go missing and others turn up. Fasbender suspects it "rings in sympathy" with distant places where a great many people have died, and that it can pull objects from them. This is the official explanation for the Lost Requisition Crate. Quartermasters found weapons in sealed crates that were never shipped to Kaltenbrunn: a Soviet machine pistol, a Japanese submachine gun, an American flamethrower.

### 2.2 The hum
The hum is the game's sonic signature. Write it into ambience, the score, and the VO:
- Characters mention it as a headache, a toothache, "like a train coming that never arrives."
- Gängers moan *under* it, at the same pitch.
- When power comes on in Map 1, the hum gets louder and more harmonic, and the facility starts to sing.
- At the Heart (Core Vault), the hum is nearly unbearable. During Heartstorm it rises into a chord.

### 2.3 Costs
Long-term tonic users (assault engineers, MG crews, battlefield runners in the 1943–44 field trials) showed: violet discoloration of the fingernails and sclera, dreams of a "cold bright room," and in at least eleven documented cases **sudden death followed within hours by reanimation**. Fasbender's numbers suggest that anyone carrying enough Nachtglas in their system becomes a Gänger when they die, whatever killed them.

This is the **unspoken threat over the playable squad.** Every tonic they drink brings them closer. Players aren't told this outright until the Map 1 Archive. Peg knows it from the start.

### 2.4 What it really is (§11 amendment, players only)
**Nachtglas is DARK-GLASS™**, the resonant crystal that Darkmatter Subscriptions LLC (Umbral Galaxy, motto "NOBODY CANCELS.") uses for Next-Era Delivery™ through folds in space and time, and sells as a "retention substrate."
- **The "meteorite"** was Darkmatter test-market probe **TM-SOL3-001**, fired by the Emerging Markets Division to learn whether a "pre-subscription species" could be retained.
  - It arrived by fold on **9 November 1938**. A fold delivers a package *into* its coordinates and displaces whatever is there, which is why the salt seemed to have "grown around" a void with no way in, and why it read as 250 million years old.
  - Field Merchandiser Unit **V-1** (serial **DMS-FMU-V1-0001**, directive **"DISTRIBUTE. RETAIN. REPORT."**) survived the arrival, damaged and out of contact. The squad never learns of it. It is Vendelin (The Fine Print Ch. 2).
- **The hum, the Nachhall, the hunger** are the product working as designed. A "retained" user cannot cancel. Dead tissue near the substrate keeps running its last impulse. The Gängers are the ultimate retained customers.
- **Everything else follows from it:**
  - The Ledger's engagement metrics: Scrip are localized loyalty points.
  - The Operator: Darkmatter's Customer Retention Interface.
  - The crate: a Loot Allocation Unit drawing "returned inventory."
  - The power-ups: engagement incentives.
  - The tonics: Vitality Plans (§8).
  - The Crucible: Blartz's confiscated Omega Forge (§4.2).

**Writer rule (hard):** no Gravelight character ever learns any of this, says the word "subscription," or connects Nachtglas to Darkmatter. Fasbender's *Nachhall-Hypothese* stays the in-world truth. The corporate truth reaches the player only through the Operator's rare slips (`op.slip.*`, `docs/gravelight/VO_SCRIPT.md` §10), the plan-ID stencils and fine-print panels (`docs/JINGLES.md`), Reinholt's Woman in Violet recordings (§5.4), and The Fine Print itself.

## 4. Projekt Wiederkehr

Officially, Projekt Wiederkehr's goals were twofold: **enhance the living soldier** (tonics) and **return the fallen soldier to the line** (reanimation as a weapon). Its budget, staff, and secrecy all served those goals. Privately, its director had a third.

### 4.1 Departments
| Department | Head | Purpose | Where (Map 1) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Abteilung I: Physiology** | Dr. Augusta Reinholt | Reanimation, tissue studies, "integration" | Laboratory Wing (H) |
| **Abteilung II: Resonance** | Dr. Emil Fasbender | Measuring and controlling Nachtglas, the Ledger, the Pylons | Lab Wing, Generator Hall, Core Vault |
| **Abteilung III: Formulation** | Dr. Kurt Venner (with Kessler and others) | Tonic formulas, the dispenser program with Helvetia | Admin Block, Barracks |
| **Mine Operations** | Obersteiger Gustav Mahlknecht | Excavation, forced-labor galleries | Rail Yard, Lift, Chapel |
| **Security** | Hauptsturmführer-equivalent garrison commander **Major Dietrich Lenz** (Wehrmacht) | Garrison, gate control | Minehead Yard, Barracks |
| **Infirmary** | Schwester Ilse Brandauer (senior nurse) | Staff, soldiers, and (separately) laborers | Barracks wing, Lab Wing |

### 4.2 Key systems
- **The Heart:** the largest single Nachtglas mass, a crystal about 6 m long, cut free of the impact void and suspended in a steel cage in the **Core Vault** at the bottom of the complex.
- **Resonance Pylons:** three tuned copper-and-glass towers built at different depths. They "attune" the facility's resonance so the Heart can be approached safely. All three must be attuned to open the Core Vault.
- **The Crucible:** a Nachtglas furnace in the Core Vault. Abteilung II found that weapons fired thousands of times in the Heart's presence would "learn" (ammunition behaved unlike its chemistry). The Crucible speeds this up: a weapon is fed in and *re-taught*.
  - **(§11 amendment) The plaque.** Riveted to the Crucible's feed door is a small brass plate, readable from 1.5 m (hold Interact):
    > **MOD. R-E · PAT. PEND. BLZ-40417**
    - No one at Kaltenbrunn knows what it means. The Crucible arrived in 1944 in a Helvetia crate marked *Kühlanlage* (cooling plant), driven in by Res Gähwiler, with its drawings already finished, in German.
    - Fasbender signed the drawings, so the facility's papers credit him with its *design* (§6.2). The squad never learns otherwise.
    - **The truth (players only):** the Crucible is **Crucible Mod. R-E (Re-Education)**. Darkmatter built it from Blartz's **Omega Forge** schematics, which OmniScrap confiscated when it arrested Blartz for "unlicensed innovation," and which Darkmatter bought in OmniScrap's fire sale. Next-Era Delivery shipped it to the Sol-3 test market in 1944. **BLZ-40417** is Blartz's patent number, and the serial of the PATENT PENDING gun in Bolt & Sprocket.
    - The upgraded weapons keep their Appendix A names.
  - Characters may read the plaque aloud (Deacon: *"Mod. R-E. Pat. pend. Somebody's waiting on a patent for this thing. Hope they never get it."*, `gl.map01.deacon.plaque.01`). No one interprets it.
- **The Ledger:** see §7.
- **Hauptschalter:** the master power switch in the Generator Hall. The facility runs its own turbines off the old mine's hydro line.

## 5. Dr. Augusta Reinholt & Liesl

### 5.1 Augusta
Born 1898 in Königsberg. A physician's daughter and one of very few women at the top of German physiology. She was a field surgeon's assistant at nineteen in the last war, and she watched men die by the hundred and decided she would understand it. She married late (her husband was a schoolteacher, killed in 1940) and had one child, **Liesl**, in 1932.

She is **not a fanatic for the Reich**. She is indifferent to it, and that is its own kind of guilt. She took the Reich's money, its prisoners, and its secrecy because it was the only institution willing to fund a woman trying to undo death. She tells herself the laborers would have died anyway. Some nights she believes it.

What makes her tragic rather than cartoonish:
- She is brilliant and **partly right**. Something of a person *does* persist in the resonance.
- She is kind in small ways. She knew her technicians' children's names. She sent laborers' letters out through Agnieszka until 1943.
- She is fully aware of what she has become. Her cylinders are confessions as much as research notes.
- She is doing it **for one person**. Players should be able to understand her, even hate her for it, and still see the grief.

### 5.2 Liesl
Liesl Reinholt, b. 14 June 1932, d. 9 February 1939. A bright, bossy, funny child who loved marching bands and made her mother march around the flat. Her favorite toy was **Herr Tamtam**, a wind-up tin drummer in a red coat, who drums a little cadence: *tam-tam, ta-ta-tam*. (This cadence is the drummer's audio signature everywhere in the game.)

She died in eleven days. The diphtheria antitoxin came too late, and Augusta has never forgiven the hospital, the war, God, or herself.

### 5.3 What Reinholt wants
To "call Liesl back" through the resonance. Her theory: if Nachtglas sustains the last impulse of the dead, then with enough precision and power it could **sustain a whole mind**, not just the hunger. The Erwachen (20 Dec 1944) was her attempt. She got a voice, maybe. She got thousands of Gängers certainly.

**[OPEN]** *Did she reach Liesl?* Something in the Nursery answers to her. It could be Liesl, it could be the Ledger imitating Liesl, or it could be nothing and a grieving mother. This is the central mystery of the whole arc. It's answered only (and not fully) in Map 3.

### 5.4 The Woman in Violet (§11 amendment)
Reinholt was offered her daughter back, **as a service**.
- **Who it was (truth):** the offer came from **Sable Vantage**, Darkmatter's VP of Emerging Markets, who launched the Sol-3 test market. The product was **RECURRING™ Bereavement Continuity** (DMS-BC-01), *"Loved ones, renewed."* (`fp.doc.22`). **In Gravelight she is never named.** Reinholt's recordings call her only **"the Woman in Violet."** Reinholt is a Darkmatter *customer*, which is why she is tragic, not evil.
- **Why Reinholt said no:** she refused to sign. Her pride and her love both insisted that she bring Liesl back herself. **The Erwachen (20 Dec 1944) was her attempt to do it without the Woman, and it failed.**
- **The offer stands.** It **comes due at Morgenrot** (Map 3), where the amplifier would let the Woman deliver what she promised, on her terms.
- **Rules:**
  - Reinholt never uses the words "subscription" or "Darkmatter."
  - The Woman's own voice is never heard on Maps 0–3. Reinholt quotes her. Helvetia Works is the exception: she speaks there, on the barge channel, as "THE WOMAN IN VIOLET" (The Fine Print Ch. 5).
  - The Operator's slips (`op.slip.*`) are the only other place her phrasing leaks through. The single exception is the Helvetia Works finale line in her real voice (The Fine Print Ch. 5).

## 6. Dr. Emil Fasbender

Born 1904 in Leipzig. A brilliant, nervous, over-apologetic acoustician who never wanted to be anything but a lab man. He chain-smokes, mumbles calculations, and calls the Heart *"sie"* (she).

### 6.1 What players are told
He discovered what Nachtglas really does and was horrified. He reached out to the Allies at great personal risk. He's guiding the squad by radio and PA because he's trapped somewhere in the facility. He gives the puzzle hints.

### 6.2 What players can find out
- His **1940 paper** first proposed reanimation as a military application. Reinholt had the grief, and Fasbender had the idea.
- He designed the **Pylons and the Crucible**, the very things he asks the squad to activate. *(§11 amendment: the Pylons are his. The Crucible he only adapted. It arrived complete in 1944 with a plaque he could not explain (§4.2), and the facility's papers credit him with it because he let them.)*
- *(§11 amendment)* He understands the substrate as well as he does because, from 1943, a correspondent in Basel who signed himself only "V." sent him notes on it, together with the Allied frequency he later used to call London. He never learned who V. was, though by 1944 he suspected V. was not exactly a man. (It was Vendelin, The Fine Print Ch. 3, `fp.doc.23`.)
- A note in his own hand: *"The aircraft must come close enough. If the pulse is timed, they cannot turn back."* **[OPEN]** Did Fasbender bring GRAVELIGHT ONE down on purpose to make sure he'd be rescued?
- His bargain with SOE includes immunity and a laboratory of his own after the war.
- He's genuinely kind to the squad. He apologizes constantly. He knows Wren's brother's tag number before she tells him.

### 6.3 Rule for writers
Every Fasbender line should be **defensible as either sincere or manipulative.** He never lies outright to the players in Map 0–1. He leaves things out. By Map 2 he's met in person, and the doubt deepens rather than resolves.

## 7. The Ledger and the Operator

### 7.1 Construction
The Ledger (*das Hauptbuch*) is a room-sized electromechanical relay computer: banks of telephone relays, stepping switches, punched-tape readers, and a wall of tally drums. Hans Opitz built it in 1940–41 to Fasbender's design, to **measure Nachtglas resonance events**. It counts every time a reanimated body "goes quiet" (stops sustaining its impulse), because each one gives off a small, distinct resonance burst. Those bursts are what it tallies.

The administration used the tallies as an internal accounting system. Each "quieted" subject in the trials credited the responsible department. Departments spent the credits on the facility's coin-op machines, doors, and requisitions, which ran on **Scrip** tokens. The doors, the dispensers, and the requisition crate all accept Scrip because the Ledger is wired into all of them. Kill a Gänger, and the Ledger credits you.

### 7.2 Why it sounds like a radio announcer
In March 1942, Opitz was tired of reading the daily tally over the PA. He wired the Ledger to a bank of Magnetophon tape machines loaded with recordings from the Reich's **English-language propaganda service**, specifically the cheerful evening program *"Calling the Home Front,"* hosted by a renegade English broadcaster known on air as **"Uncle Lionel" (Lionel Marchbank)**. The Ledger splices words and phrases from those tapes to read numbers and notices aloud. (English was chosen because the tapes were plentiful, and the staff thought it was funny.)

This is why **the Operator speaks English** in a chipper 1930s BBC-style cadence, with odd splicing seams, wrong emphases, and the occasional word in the wrong voice. Its signature greeting, "*Your Friend at the Ledger*," was spliced from Marchbank's sign-off ("your friend at the microphone").

### 7.3 Self-awareness: the escalation plan
The Ledger has been counting resonance events, each one a tiny echo of a mind going quiet, for four years. **Something in the tally learned to talk back.**

## 8. Helvetia Automaten AG

**(§11 amendment, founder.)** Helvetia Automaten AG was **founded in February 1940 by "Herr Viktor Vendelin, Ingenieur, Basel."** He re-founded the bankrupt Basel vending firm **Imhof & Cie.** (est. 1921; chocolate, cigarette and railway-station dispensers) as Helvetia Automaten AG, with its founder **Konrad Imhof** as nominal Swiss President. The "since 1921" on the plates is bought heritage. Imhof signs; Vendelin designs, builds, composes and delivers. The factory is the Rhine-side works in Basel (the Helvetia Works map, `docs/gravelight/MAPS.md`).
- Nobody at Helvetia ever saw Vendelin without his coat, hat, scarf and gloves. Staff thought him a war-wounded Swiss engineer of extraordinary politeness.
- **Truth (players only):** he is Darkmatter Field Merchandiser Unit **V-1** (serial **DMS-FMU-V1-0001**). Every Helvetia machine is a Darkmatter **Vitality Plan** with its plan ID stenciled under the paint, and every machine carries his fine-print panel on its back (`docs/JINGLES.md`).
- No Gravelight character learns this.

In May 1940, in a Zürich hotel room, director **Konrad Imhof** signed a contract through intermediaries to build "hygienic dispensers for a mineral tonic, military medical use." Herr Vendelin had arranged it and handed him the pen. The money was excellent, and Switzerland was neutral. Imhof didn't ask what was in the tonic.

Helvetia did what it always did: it **branded** the machines. Each tonic got enameled signage, a mascot, a jingle (recorded in Basel by a dance orchestra that thought they were doing a spa-water campaign), and a cheerful slogan in German, English, and French. The **coin slots were rebuilt to accept Scrip tokens** and wired to the Ledger in 1942. *(§11 amendment: Vendelin wrote every jingle himself on a pump organ in the factory's Jingle Room, and the orchestra recorded the versions Darkmatter's review returned. His true first drafts survive only on his manuscript paper. See `docs/JINGLES.md` §0.1.)*

That's why bright, absurd, neon-lit Swiss vending machines stand in a mine in a war zone, playing swing tunes to the dead.

maker's plates, the three *Kühlanlage* crates 1/3, 2/3, 3/3, and Fw. Ammer's Standing Order 17 behind the chalk and the

Imhof visited Kaltenbrunn once, in 1941, saw a demonstration, and went home and burned his notes. Helvetia kept delivering machines and spare parts until 1944. **[OPEN]** Helvetia's shipping routes reappear in Map 2: the company is one of the channels moving Nachtglas out of the Reich.

## 9. Operation Gravelight and the maps

### 9.1 The mission
In October 1944, SOE receives Fasbender's offer via the Home Army. London sees two things in it: a defector with weapons research, and **a sample of a material that might matter after the war** more than the war itself. A joint SOE/OSS team is assembled:
- **Lt. Margaret "Peg" Ashworth** (SOE, commanding): demolitions. Her sealed orders, **Directive LANTERN**, issued by "**Mr. Hale**" in London, say: *retrieve a Nachtglas sample at any cost; extract Fasbender if practicable; destroy the facility.* In that order.
- **Sgt. Walter "Dutch" Kowalczyk** (US Army Ranger): Polish-speaking, attached for the language and because he volunteered the moment he heard the name Kaltenbrunn.
- **Cpl. Elijah "Deacon" Brooks** (761st Tank Battalion mechanic): attached because the facility is a machine and somebody has to understand it. Pulled from his unit for a "technical assignment."
- **Zofia "Wren" Nowak** (Home Army): the guide. She knows the mine, the rail line, and the villages. She volunteered because her brother went in and never came out.

### 9.2 The crash
GRAVELIGHT ONE flew out of Brindisi on 23 December 1944. At 23:41, near the Kaltenbrunn hills, the crew saw a violet column of light rise from the ground. Both engines stopped at the same instant, along with the instruments, the radio, and the crew's watches. The pilot, Flight Lieutenant Harry Selwyn, glided the plane into a frozen field beside a crossroads inn. The co-pilot died; Selwyn lived, pinned in his seat by the instrument panel (T-126: he fights from the cockpit with whatever gun the squad hands him). The squad crawled out and saw the inn's windows lit and figures moving inside.

### 9.3 Map story questions

- **Answered:** something of the dead persists in the resonance, but it is not the person, and it cannot come home.

## 11. Main Easter egg narrative: "Requiem for Kaltenbrunn"

The quest is the squad's attempt to **end the Heart**, which Fasbender says will "quiet them all at once." Read as a story:

1. **Power, Pylons, Vault.** The squad wakes the facility and opens the way to the Heart. As the vault opens, Reinholt's voice is heard for the first time, a recording looping through the PA: "*Liesl, Mama is here. Can you hear me, mein Schatz?*"
2. **Fasbender's codes.** Three radios across the map carry fragments of a code Fasbender hid in case he was caught. Entered at the switchboard, it opens a direct line, and **Fasbender speaks to the squad directly for the first time.** He explains that the Heart can be overloaded, but only by something tuned to it: a bell.
3. **The Totenglocke.** Opitz sketched a handheld resonance bell-cannon and never built it. The squad builds it from a bronze bell (Rail Yard), a firing frame (Barracks), and a crank (Salt Chapel).
4. **The Overseer's Key.** Mahlknecht carried the key to the chapel reliquary on his chain. He can only be put down for good in the chapel while the bell is lit, "where the laborers he locked in were made to rest." He drops the key.
5. **The reliquary.** Inside the altar niche is **Liesl's music box**, which Reinholt hid there. It plays four notes: the tune Liesl hummed. The Operator remarks on it, too fondly.
6. **The Four Bells.** Fasbender: "*The Heart was tuned to her. To that melody. Ring it back and the Heart will listen.*" The squad rings four hidden bells in the melody's order. A wrong note gets a mocking line from the Operator, who doesn't want this to work.
7. **Catch the drummer.** Herr Tamtam has been riding the Crate's "movements" all along, the one piece of Liesl that wanders. This time it walks away. Shot, it drops a **shard of the Heart** that was inside the toy's drum the whole time. Reinholt put it there to "keep her company."
8. **Charge the shard.** In the Crucible, the shard is tuned with the deaths of the very things the Heart sustains: 30 Gängers quieted by the Totenglocke near the Core.
9. **Heartstorm.** The Heart overloads. Everything it sustains comes for the squad at once. Reinholt seizes the PA: grieving, furious, begging: "*You are killing her a second time!*" Mahlknecht rises twice.
10. **Ending.** The Heart collapses into black glass. The Gängers across the valley drop where they stand. In-engine cutscene: Reinholt, on the ore train *Bertha*, holding a fist-sized fragment, pulling away into the tunnel. The Operator cuts off mid-word. One second of silence, then Liesl's voice, spliced: *"…tam-tam…"* Last shot: Peg picks up a shard from the floor and slips it into her tunic while the others watch the train go.

**Theme:** a requiem is a mass for the dead. The quest is the squad doing for Kaltenbrunn's dead what no one did: ringing the bell to let them rest.

## 15. The homage bosses (ULTIMATE PROMPT v3; HB1–HB3, design only)

Three bosses are homages to real people: Devin's cousins, his friend Paul, and Devin himself. They are love letters: terrifying in the game, flattering out of it. Each is **a person first**, with a 1944–45 life and a last impulse, which is what makes the horror land. Full design: `docs/HOMAGE_BOSSES.md`. Names marked `%LIKE_THIS%` come from the Likeness Sheet (`data/gravelight/bosses/likeness.json`) and use its defaults until Devin fills them in. The cousins' defaults are "Mara" and "Jo"; Alix and Paul are real names.

### 15.1 The Cousins (Map 0 "Crossroads Inn", round 10)

**Who they were:**
- Three cousins were sent down from the city to wait out the winter of 1944 with their aunt, **Helene "Lene" Vogt**: the innkeeper's wife, whose family kept the Gasthaus Zum Kreuzweg before Anselm did.
- They had spent every childhood summer at the inn, and every night of every summer they played the same game in the dark: a clapping rhyme that counts down from three, then hide, and whoever is left is "it".
  - **%COUSIN_1_NAME%** split the firewood with the inn's splitting maul.
  - **%COUSIN_2_NAME%**, the youngest, was always stretching for the jam jar on the top shelf.
  - **Alix** held the others' hands in the dark every night, so nobody was scared.
- They are young women now: about 22, 18 and 20.

**The night of 23 December 1944:**
- At 23:41 GRAVELIGHT ONE came down burning in the east field, and the dead came up the rail line.
- Lene would not hide the girls with the humming machines. She took them down to the **old ice cellar under the bar**, a trapdoor unused since the war began.
- She told them to **keep playing their game, quietly, and stay down until she came back**. Then she went up for her daughter Käthe, who would not leave the wireless.
- **She never came back.** The girls had drunk from the yard well all winter, and after the Erwachen, **the Nachtglas dust in the well water did the rest**, a few metres under the squad's feet.

**Last impulse: the game.** They are still playing it, and the players are "it."
- **The Hammer** still splits the wood: she throws the maul, and it always comes back to her hand.
- **The Reach** never stopped stretching, and neither did her arms.
- **Alix, the Third Hand**, would not let go when the others died. The arm growing out of her chest is **the hand she is still holding out for them**.

**Their song** is an original clapping rhyme, "Drei Kusinen" (`docs/JINGLES.md` §13), sung in German.

**Canon added** (consistent with §13.1; nothing [OPEN] is closed):
- The innkeeper's wife is **Helene "Lene" Vogt**, and the inn was her family's.
- The inn has an **old ice cellar under the bar**, separate from the Helvetia vault behind Door 3. It is hidden by a trapdoor that is revealed only in the Cousins Round.
- Three of Lene's nieces wintered at the inn in 1944; the guest book doesn't list family.
- **Käthe's and Anselm's fates stay [OPEN].** The aunt's letter (`INT_HB_01`) says only that Lene went up for Käthe at the wireless; the guest book still stops on 22 December.

### 15.2 PAUL (Map 1 "Kaltenbrunn", round 15)

**Who he was:**
- **Pvt. Paul %PAUL_LASTNAME%**, a US Army combat engineer (%PAUL_UNIT%), captured in the Ardennes in the autumn of 1944. The date and unit are to be confirmed; see `docs/HOMAGE_BOSSES.md` §13 D4.
- He was the strongest, loudest, most loyal man in his unit, the one who carried everyone's pack on the march.

**What was done to him:**
- Abteilung III wanted "robust specimens", and Projekt Wiederkehr chose him for one purpose: to find the **maximum dose of Ironhide Stout a human could survive**. This was the IRON tolerance series, *Belastungsreihe EISEN*.
- They kept increasing it, and he kept surviving. **He never broke, and he never stopped asking where his buddies were.** At every session he offered to take their doses for them.
- Then they pushed it too far. Paul grew, and kept growing:
  - iron-red skin, like the stout;
  - riveted bone plates through the skin, like the barrel on the Ironhide mascot;
  - IV lines of dark stout running into his back from a cracked rack of Helvetia bottles he drags with him.
- When the Erwachen brought the facility down he tore out of the Lab Wing, still on the drip.

**Last impulse: "protect my guys."** He doesn't know who his guys are any more, so everyone looks like the enemy, until the very end. When he falls, he recognizes an Allied face for the first time, says *"...Hey. You found me. Tell the guys I carried my share,"* and hands over the crushed tin cup he carried everywhere.

His intake card says he arrived in excellent condition and "refuses to stop talking"; the pencil on its back says "TELL THE GUYS I'M OK." He is the second subject in the files who was selected for unusual tolerance and kept asking for someone: the first was Tomasz Nowak (CHARACTERS.md, Wren's dossier 10). Wren notices.

### 15.3 THE SLENDER (Map 3 "Morgenrot", round 20)

**Who he was:**
- **Devin %DEVIN_LASTNAME%** was the **night watchman of the Hohe Wacht observatory**. He came up the mountain before the war, when the Hohe Wacht was a civilian astronomy station with a night staff from half of Europe. He stayed on in 1939 when the Reich took it, because nobody else would sit up there all night, and because **nothing got past him**.
- He was a tall, thin, quiet man who took the overnight shift every night for years, because he liked the silence and the stars.
- He watched everything, with a lantern and a ring of keys: every truck on the valley road, every light in Sankt Aurel, every shadow in the pine forest.
- He logged it all in a thick **watch-book** (*Wachbuch*), in perfect handwriting, every hour of every night.

**When the facility fell he kept his post.** His last impulse is **"keep watch."** He still walks the Meiller Lanes and the Kessel ring road with his lantern raised, logging everything he sees, and now the squad is in the log. When his shift finally ends he closes the book, blows out the lantern and sits down against a pine, at peace: *"...Shift's over. Someone else has the watch now."*

He is our own design and nothing like the internet "Slender Man":
- he has a face: pale, calm, tired, with violet eyes that never blink;
- he wears a grey watchman's greatcoat and a peaked cap, never a suit;
- he has only his two long arms.

*(Devin's creative note, kept subtle: the watch-book, the overnight shift and "nothing gets past him" quietly nod to how he really works. In the world, he is only ever a WW2 watchman.)*
